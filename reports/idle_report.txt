Protocol Distribution:
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The traffic breaks down roughly as follows (by bytes):

  - About 38% of traffic is TLS — encrypted data sent over TCP
  - About 43% is QUIC — a newer encrypted protocol, mostly used by LinkedIn here
  - DNS (name lookups) accounts for about 1.2%
  - SSDP (TV discovery broadcasts) is about 2.4%
  - Everything else — mDNS, ARP, ICMPv6, TP-Link — is under 1% each

Nearly all application-layer traffic is encrypted. No plaintext HTTP was observed. 
All encrypted sessions appear to use TLS 1.3, the current standard.

Also worth noting: about 47% of frames carry a VLAN tag (a network label added by switches or access points). 
This is more than you'd typically expect on a basic home network and may suggest a managed switch or 
VLAN-capable router is in use — though this doesn't change what the traffic contains.

Inference and Conclusion:
-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  DEVICES ON THE NETWORK
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  At least three devices were active during this capture.

  The main computer (192.168.1.9) generated most of the traffic. Based on
  its use of QUIC/HTTP3, it's likely running a modern browser such as Chrome, Edge, or a recent version of Firefox.

  The LG Smart TV (192.168.1.2) was in standby — not streaming, just periodically announcing its presence to 
  the local network via SSDP and mDNS every 90 seconds or so. 
  Its model (UQ8050PSB) was visible in the packet data.

  A third device (identifiable only by a link-local IPv6 address) also looked up LinkedIn at the start of the 
  capture, suggesting it may have had the site open as well. Its type couldn't be determined from this capture 
  alone.

  WHY "IDLE" STILL PRODUCES TRAFFIC
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  Even with no active user interaction, the network wasn't quiet. Several things were running in the background:

  LinkedIn appeared to reconnect to its servers roughly every 60 seconds.
  This is consistent with how apps maintain a live connection for notifications
  — the regular spikes in the IO graph match this cycle closely.

  Spotify made a brief check-in at around 197 seconds despite no apparent playback. 
  This is typical behaviour for the desktop or mobile app keeping its session alive.

  The LG TV broadcast its presence on the local network every ~90 seconds, which is standard for WebOS TVs in 
  standby.

  The router and computer exchanged small address-resolution messages (ARP and ICMPv6) throughout — routine 
  network maintenance.

  This is fairly normal for a modern device. "Idle" for a user doesn't mean idle for the software running in 
  the background.

  THE UNKNOWN DOMAIN: tzm.protechts.net
  ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  The one finding that stands out is the connection to tzm.protechts.net,
  which resolved to a Google Cloud IP (34.36.73.246).

  What the capture shows:
    - Around 177 seconds in, the client looked up tzm.protechts.net and immediately opened two simultaneous 
    encrypted connections to it.
    - Both connections completed successfully and stayed open for about 75 seconds.
    - The server closed both connections (not the client).
    - After that, the client kept probing the closed connections for another ~45 seconds, suggesting it 
    expected them to remain open longer.
    - Total data exchanged was small — around 11.6 KB combined.
    - This happened automatically, with no obvious user action triggering it.

  What's uncertain:
    - The traffic is fully encrypted, so the content can't be read.
    - "protechts" doesn't match any widely known service, CDN, or OS component.
    - It could be a legitimate background application — endpoint management software, a corporate security agent, 
    or a less common SaaS tool.
    - It could also be something less expected. Without knowing what's installed on the computer, it's hard to say.

  The connection itself doesn't look alarming in terms of volume, but the domain is unrecognised and the 
  behaviour (automatic, parallel, server-timed) is worth a closer look. Checking what's running on 192.168.1.9 
  — installed applications, browser extensions, startup processes — would be the natural next step.

  OVERALL PICTURE
  ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  This is a quiet five-minute window on what appears to be a home or small office network. 
  The traffic is dominated by LinkedIn's background activity on the main computer, with minor contributions
  from Spotify, Google, and the LG TV. Almost everything is encrypted, which is expected and normal.

  The only item that doesn't have an obvious explanation is tzm.protechts.net.
  Every other external address in the capture — LinkedIn, Google, Spotify, Cloudflare, Microsoft — is a 
  recognisable service. That one isn't, and it would be worth identifying before treating this capture as fully understood.
