Inference and Conclusion:
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  WHAT WAS RUNNING DURING THIS CAPTURE
  ────────────────────────────────────
  The traffic pattern strongly suggests YouTube playing a video in the browser, with LinkedIn and possibly Gmail open in background tabs.

  YouTube's video delivery servers account for ~90% of all traffic. The DNS queries for googlevideo.com appear within the first 8 seconds —
  before most other activity — suggesting the video was already loaded when the capture started. The QUIC stream to 2404:a800:6:285::d ran 
  at a sustained rate for the full 5 minutes with gradual fluctuations typical of adaptive video buffering. The ad-related domains 
  (googlesyndication, doubleclick) that appeared early are consistent with a YouTube page with ads loading, not necessarily with an ad being
  watched — they load as part of the page infrastructure.

  LinkedIn's background session continued exactly as seen in the idle and browsing captures — the same 60-second re-query cycle, the same 
  server address, consistent with a pinned tab or always-on session.

  THE TP-LINK PATTERN — A CLARIFICATION
  ─────────────────────────────────────
  In the idle and browsing captures, TP-Link discovery commands appeared to come from 192.168.1.2 (the LG TV), which seemed unusual. 
  The same is true here, but with a notable addition: the TP-Link commands are specifically requesting device status ({get_sysinfo} 
  and {get_realtime} for energy monitoring), and they are sent every ~25 seconds like clockwork throughout the capture. 
  This regularity and the specific command format suggest the TV may have a TP-Link smart home integration built into its WebOS 
  platform, or the traffic may originate from another integrated smart-home component associated with the same environment. 
  Either way, something on the network is actively polling a TP-Link smart plug or energy monitor at a consistent interval across all 
  three captures.

  192.168.1.11 — LOOKING FOR A CHROMECAST
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  This device sent repeated mDNS queries for _googlecast._tcp.local — the service type used by Chromecast devices to advertise themselves 
  on a local network. It also sent SSDP searches. No device responded to these queries in the capture. This suggests either there is no 
  Chromecast on the network, or it's on a different network segment and not reachable via multicast. The device identity is still unclear 
  — it could be an Android phone, an Android TV box, or any device with Google Home or Google TV software that periodically scans for 
  Chromecast receivers.

  tzm.protechts.net — NOW SEEN IN ALL THREE CAPTURES
  ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
  The domain appeared in the idle capture (at ~178s), the browsing capture (at ~236s), and now here (at ~271s). Across all three captures, 
  the pattern is consistent:
    - Three parallel DNS queries each time
    - Two simultaneous TCP connections opened immediately after
    - Small amounts of data exchanged (11–15 KB each time)
    - Server closes the connections after 30–75 seconds
    - Timing varies but tends to appear in the latter half of each capture

  The fact that it appeared across three separate sessions — one idle, one active browsing, one video playback — makes it very unlikely to be
  triggered by a specific user action or website. It appears to run on its own schedule, independently of what else is happening. Whether that 
  makes it a legitimate background service or something worth investigating is a question the packet data alone cannot answer, since all traffic 
  is encrypted. The recommendation from the previous reports still stands: checking what is installed and running on 192.168.1.9 would be the
  clearest way to identify it.

  OVERALL PICTURE
  ────────────────
  This is the simplest of the three captures to characterise. One application — most likely YouTube — dominated the session, with everything else
  running at low volume in the background. The traffic pattern is smooth and sustained rather than bursty, which is consistent with video streaming. 
  The devices and background services seen here are consistent with the other two captures, with no new unknown destinations introduced except for 
  the continued presence of tzm.protechts.net.

  Across all three captures (idle, browsing, app usage), the network picture is of a single primary computer with LinkedIn and Spotify running 
  persistently in the background, a smart TV in standby, an unidentified device sending SSDP broadcasts, and a Chromecast-seeking device that
  can't find what it's looking for. The one recurring item that doesn't fit neatly into that picture is tzm.protechts.net, which has now appeared 
  in every single capture.
