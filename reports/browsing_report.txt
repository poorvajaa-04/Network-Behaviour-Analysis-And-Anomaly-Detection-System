Protocol Distribution:
-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Traffic breakdown by bytes across the capture:

  - About 38% is TLS — standard encrypted traffic over TCP
  - About 42% is QUIC — the newer encrypted protocol used by Google and YouTube
  - DNS (name lookups) accounts for roughly 1.3%
  - SSDP and TP-Link discovery broadcasts make up under 0.1% each
  - A small amount of plaintext HTTP was observed — discussed below

Nearly everything is encrypted. All TLS sessions appear to use TLS 1.3. 
QUIC traffic is notably higher here than in the idle capture, which is consistent with active browsing on Google and 
YouTube properties.

One difference from idle: a handful of unencrypted HTTP requests appear in this capture. 
These are not page visits — they are certificate status checks (OCSP) and a Microsoft BITS download request 
(a background Windows update component). No sensitive page content was transmitted in plaintext.

VLAN-tagged frames again make up a large portion — around 53% — similar to the idle capture, and likely for the same 
infrastructure reason.

As a point of comparison with the idle capture: total bytes here are ~85 MB versus ~342 KB in the idle file. 
This is roughly 250 times more data, reflecting active browsing rather than a quiet background state.

Inference and Conclusion:

  WHAT HAPPENED DURING THIS CAPTURE
  ─────────────────────────────────
  This is an active browsing session, not a quiet background state. Based on
  the DNS query timeline and conversation data, the likely sequence of events:

    0–90s    : YouTube was open and loading (possibly watching a video).
               Google Docs and Google Drive were also active — possibly
               open in separate tabs. ProctorAuth loaded during this window
               as well, which is worth noting (see below).

    90–150s  : A pause. Little traffic. The user may have stopped interacting
               with the browser, or was reading without triggering new loads.

    150–180s : New tabs opened — GitHub appeared first, then LinkedIn was
               re-queried (persistent session), then bettermindlabs.org.

    180–240s : Sessions from the previous phase continued in the background.
               Around 236s, tzm.protechts.net connected again.

    240–300s : YCombinator's forum (Bookface) opened, generating the largest
               data burst in the capture. A Wix-hosted website also loaded,
               bringing with it a range of analytics and tracking scripts.

  Three local IP addresses appeared that were not in the idle capture:

  192.168.1.5 sent SSDP M-SEARCH broadcasts every ~60 seconds throughout the session. This is a device actively looking for UPnP 
  services on the network. It could be a smart home controller, a media app, or another computer. Its identity couldn't be confirmed

  192.168.1.7 sent a handful of packets, all outbound. Possibly a device that woke briefly, sent some background traffic, and went quiet.

  192.168.1.11 appeared once. Not enough data to characterise it.

  PROCTORAUTH — EXAM PROCTORING SOFTWARE
  ──────────────────────────────────────
  Two domains — gbl.proctorauth.com and cdn.proctorauth.com — were queried within the first 15 seconds of the capture, alongside YouTube 
  and Google Docs. ProctorAuth and Proctor.io are services used by universities and online exam platforms to monitor students during 
  remote assessments. They typically run as a browser extension or web app that checks system conditions (screen, camera, tab activity) 
  during a test.

  Finding ProctorAuth traffic alongside YouTube and Google Docs is unusual, but not necessarily suspicious. It may simply indicate that a 
  browser tab, extension, or background session related to an online assessment platform was active during the capture. The packet data 
  alone is not sufficient to determine the exact context of use.

  BETTERMINDLABS.ORG
  ──────────────────
  This domain appeared around 166 seconds and generated ~406 KB of traffic across two sessions. It resolves to Google Cloud. The site loaded 
  PostHog analytics, Google Analytics, Google Tag Manager, and a Sentry error reporting endpoint — all standard embedded tools on modern websites.
  The Sentry endpoint ID (o4506690008121344) is specific to one Sentry account, meaning the website is a real, actively maintained web app.
  Nothing about the traffic itself is unusual — it's just a site that isn't widely recognisable by name.

  tzm.protechts.net — SAME UNKNOWN DOMAIN, AGAIN
  ──────────────────────────────────────────────
  The same domain from the idle capture appeared again here, at around 236 seconds. The behaviour was structurally identical: two simultaneous
  connections opened, data exchanged, server closed the connections. This time the session was shorter (~30 seconds vs ~75 seconds) and the data
  volume was smaller (~14 KB vs ~11.6 KB). Its reappearance in an active browsing session, a few minutes after the idle capture, suggests it fires
  on a schedule or in response to some trigger — rather than being a one-off.

  The recommendation from the idle capture still applies: identifying what application on 192.168.1.9 connects to this domain would be the most
  direct way to resolve whether it's expected behaviour or not.

  TRACKING AND ANALYTICS LOAD

  Several analytics and tracking scripts were loaded as a side effect of visiting various sites — particularly toward the end of the capture. These

  rather than signs of anything unusual, but they do illustrate how much third-party data collection happens in the background of ordinary browsing.

  OVERALL PICTURE
  ───────────────
  This is a clearly active browsing session. The user appears to have had YouTube, Google Docs/Drive, GitHub, YCombinator's forum, and at least one
  Wix-hosted site open, along with LinkedIn running in the background. Traffic volume is dramatically higher than the idle capture, and the pattern 
  reflects real user actions rather than automated background processes.

  The main items that stand out are: ProctorAuth loading alongside YouTube and Docs (context-dependent whether this is notable), and tzm.protechts.net
  reappearing for the second time. Everything else observed — including the analytics scripts and Amazon S3 requests — is consistent with normal modern
  web browsing behaviour.
